## 1. Information regulation changes not only speakers’ incentives but also how Bayesian audiences interpret and act on the statements that remain

**Location:** Introduction, printed pp. 1-3 (PDF pp. 1-3)

Professors Yonathan A. Arbel and Murat C. Mungan claim, in “Regulating Information with Bayesian Audiences” on pages 1–3, that analysis of false-statement regulation has neglected the audience. A listener rationally adjusts the credibility assigned to speech when legal sanctions change which speakers are willing to speak, so regulation reshapes the informational environment as well as the conduct of speakers and targets. This is significant because the social harm from false speech depends on belief and action, not falsity alone. It connects to Bayesian updating, information regulation, audience effects, signaling, credibility, and behavioral law and economics.

**Evidence anchor:** The introduction identifies an audience gap and explains that legal strictness directly affects public beliefs and actions.

**Boundary:** The paper models a rational representative audience and does not claim that every real listener updates beliefs perfectly or identically.

**Connections:** Bayesian updating; information regulation; audience effects; signaling; credibility; behavioral law and economics

## 2. A three-party Bayesian game captures how a privately informed speaker influences an audience’s decision about a target under threat of litigation

**Location:** Introduction and Model, printed pp. 5-10 (PDF pp. 5-10)

Professors Yonathan A. Arbel and Murat C. Mungan claim, in “Regulating Information with Bayesian Audiences” on pages 1–2 and 5–10, that a Bayesian game can formalize the interaction among a speaker, a good or bad target, and an audience deciding whether to interact with that target. The speaker observes the target’s type and may disparage; the audience updates and acts; the target may sue after a lost interaction. This is significant because each player anticipates the others, making the credibility of speech an equilibrium product of law. It connects to Perfect Bayesian equilibrium, private information, strategic communication, defamation litigation, reputation, and sequential games.

**Evidence anchor:** The model specifies the speaker’s message, the audience’s interaction choice, the target’s suit, and belief-consistency requirements.

**Boundary:** The baseline reduces targets to two types, audiences to one representative actor, and statements to disparaging or non-disparaging messages.

**Connections:** Perfect Bayesian equilibrium; private information; strategic communication; defamation litigation; reputation; sequential games

## 4. The level of expected damages is a useful policy lever because it jointly shapes the target’s incentive to sue and the speaker’s incentive to disparage

**Location:** Model, printed pp. 7-8 (PDF pp. 7-8)

Professors Yonathan A. Arbel and Murat C. Mungan claim, in “Regulating Information with Bayesian Audiences” on pages 2 and 7–8, that damages operationalize the strictness of information law. A target sues when expected recovery exceeds litigation cost, while a speaker disparages only when the private gain from blocking an interaction exceeds expected liability and cost. Judicial error enters through type-dependent probabilities of plaintiff victory. This is significant because a nominal damages amount works through several strategic thresholds rather than a single deterrence response. It connects to optimal damages, litigation costs, expected liability, private enforcement, judicial error, and deterrence theory.

**Evidence anchor:** The paper defines damages, litigation cost, and type-contingent win probabilities and derives target and speaker incentives from them.

**Boundary:** Damages are the model’s principal lever; real information regimes also use injunctions, procedures, insurance, and nonmonetary sanctions.

## 5. Stricter law can make surviving disparagement more credible and therefore more damaging, so even a good target may prefer a laxer regime

**Location:** Introduction, printed pp. 2 (PDF pp. 2)

Professors Yonathan A. Arbel and Murat C. Mungan claim, in “Regulating Information with Bayesian Audiences” on page 2, that strict defamation law makes a negative statement a costlier signal. A Bayesian audience consequently treats the statement as more reliable; a sufficiently motivated liar can exploit that trust, causing a false accusation that survives deterrence to have greater impact. A good target may therefore prefer lower damages even though they reduce recovery if suit succeeds. **Evidence anchor:** The introduction explains that strict law increases the cost and perceived reliability of speech, which a determined false speaker may abuse.

**Boundary:** The preference result is possible under specified equilibrium conditions; it is not a claim that every good target always prefers lax law.
