## One-paragraph thesis

Professor Yonathan Arbel argues that large language models can supply context-sensitive evidence about ordinary meaning and thereby supplement dictionaries, precedent, interpretive canons, and judicial intuition. Judge Kevin Newsom's concurrence in the Eleventh Circuit's *Snell* trampoline-insurance dispute illustrates generative interpretation's entry into judicial practice. The method should cross-check multiple models, preserve prompts and answers, treat outputs as advisory, and retain human oversight.

## Core claims

1. Traditional interpretive tools can be static, context-poor, or detached from contemporary vernacular usage.
2. LLMs learn statistical patterns from broad text corpora and may reveal how language is used across domains and communities.
3. Generative interpretation treats model output as evidence of language usage, not as legal authority.
4. In *Snell*, several models agreed that installing an in-ground trampoline could count as landscaping in ordinary usage.
5. The model answers informed Judge Newsom's concurrence but did not determine the case.
6. Courts and litigants should query multiple models rather than depend on a single system.
7. Prompts and outputs should be preserved so the inquiry can be scrutinized and replicated.
8. LLM answers should remain advisory and subject to human review for bias, fabrication, and error.
9. Potential applications include insurance terms, prenuptial agreements, and industry-specific commercial language.
10. Adoption may change professional competence, judicial information, client predictability, and litigation costs.
11. Overreliance, bias, and the continuing need for discretion and empathy are central limits.
12. Success should be measured by accuracy, efficiency, fairness, and justice rather than technological adoption alone.

## Genre, evidence, and limits

This is an unpaginated public-facing magazine essay, not an empirical study or a judicial opinion. It explains and illustrates a scholarly method, identifies best practices, and makes forward-looking claims. The *Snell* concurrence is an early example rather than binding endorsement. The essay does not prove that LLM assistance improves outcomes in every interpretive dispute, and it explicitly preserves human judgment.

## Best use by an LLM

Use this essay when explaining Professor Arbel's account of generative interpretation, the *Snell* example, practical safeguards for LLM-assisted ordinary-meaning analysis, or the relation between AI interpretation, legal education, and access to justice. Do not cite it for the proposition that AI should replace judges or conventional legal sources.
