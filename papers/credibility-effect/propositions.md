## 1. Defamation doctrine’s bilateral balance omits audiences even though their belief converts publication into reputational harm

**Location:** Section 1, Introduction, printed pp. 417-418 (PDF pp. 1-2)

Professor Yonathan A. Arbel claims, in “The Credibility Effect: Defamation Law and Audiences” on pages 417–418, that courts conventionally balance the interests of speakers against the reputations of their targets. That bilateral model neglects a third party—the audience—even though defamatory speech affects reputation only when listeners believe and act on it. This is significant because the legal standard may change the causal process that produces harm, not merely constrain the speaker after the fact. It connects to defamation, reputational harm, audience theory, bilateral balancing, the marketplace of ideas, and third-party effects.

**Evidence anchor:** The introduction describes the Supreme Court’s speaker-target balance and identifies audiences as a fundamental but neglected part of the marketplace of ideas.

**Boundary:** The article tests selected audience responses and does not claim to model every interaction among speakers, targets, publishers, and listeners.

**Connections:** defamation; reputational harm; audience theory; bilateral balancing; marketplace of ideas; third-party effects

## 2. Effective defamation law can make speech more credible because audiences infer that legal sanctions deter false statements

**Location:** Section 1, The Credibility Hypothesis, printed pp. 418 (PDF pp. 2)

Professor Yonathan A. Arbel claims, in “The Credibility Effect: Defamation Law and Audiences” on page 418, that audiences can reason from a speaker’s legal incentives. If false accusations are likely to trigger costly liability, listeners may infer that a published assertion is more likely true; if liability is ineffective, they may discount the assertion and rely on other credibility cues. This is significant because deterrence produces an epistemic spillover—greater trust in the speech that remains. It connects to costly signaling, deterrence, Bayesian audiences, legal incentives, source credibility, and public trust.

**Evidence anchor:** The introduction summarizes theoretical models in which effective liability reduces expected lying and thereby increases audience credence.

**Boundary:** The mechanism can operate only to the extent that audiences perceive legal exposure directly or learn its effects through intermediaries and experience.

**Connections:** costly signaling; deterrence; Bayesian audiences; legal incentives; source credibility; public trust

## 3. The credibility effect helps when reports are true but can magnify misinformation and stigmatize innocent targets

**Location:** Abstract and Section 1, printed pp. 417-418 (PDF pp. 1-2)

Professor Yonathan A. Arbel claims, in “The Credibility Effect: Defamation Law and Audiences” on pages 417–418, that law-induced trust has asymmetric consequences. Increased credibility improves reliance on accurate reports, but when a false report survives the liability regime, the same trust can intensify reputational harm; people may also infer guilt when an innocent target does not sue. This is significant because a policy that improves average source credibility can still create serious errors for particular targets. It connects to misinformation, false positives, stigma, residual falsehoods, public trust, and unintended consequences.

**Evidence anchor:** The abstract and introduction contrast the desirable effect on truthful reports with perverse effects on misinformation and nonsuing targets.

**Boundary:** The net welfare effect depends on the prevalence of true and false reports, the strength of deterrence, and the behavioral stakes in a particular domain.

**Connections:** misinformation; false positives; stigma; residual falsehoods; public trust; unintended consequences

## 4. Credibility effects make stricter law and deregulation potential trust-management tools pointing in opposite directions

Arbel claims, in “The Credibility Effect: Defamation Law and Audiences” on pages 418–419, that policymakers worried about declining trust in mainstream media might use stronger defamation accountability to raise credibility. Conversely, those seeking to reduce the perceived authority of tabloids or social media might find that weaker legal screening encourages skepticism. This is significant because the desirable legal direction depends partly on the preexisting and desired level of trust in the relevant source domain. **Evidence anchor:** The introduction describes stronger law as a possible trust-enhancing tool and weaker law as a possible means of discounting problematic sources.

**Boundary:** Manipulating trust through liability also affects speech supply, victim remedies, and error costs, so credibility cannot be the sole policy objective.

## 5. The credibility effect faces informational, legal-consciousness, and cognitive objections that require empirical testing

**Location:** Section 1, Objections to Realism, printed pp. 419 (PDF pp. 3)

Professor Yonathan A. Arbel claims, in “The Credibility Effect: Defamation Law and Audiences” on page 419, that laypeople may not know complex defamation rules, may not think about law while consuming news, and may lack the cognitive capacity to infer truth from a speaker’s incentives. The fundamental attribution problem and documented failures of Bayesian reasoning make the last objection especially serious. This is significant because a theoretically rational signaling effect may have little real-world force if ordinary audiences cannot or do not perform the inference. It connects to legal consciousness, bounded rationality, legal knowledge, fundamental attribution error, Bayesian reasoning, and empirical validation.

**Evidence anchor:** The introduction identifies three challenges and relates the cognitive objection to base-rate neglect and dispositional attribution.

**Boundary:** The categories overlap, and an observed effect in a prompted experiment does not by itself resolve how each obstacle operates in everyday life.

**Connections:** legal consciousness; bounded rationality; legal knowledge; fundamental attribution error; Bayesian reasoning; empirical validation

## 27. The standard vindication rationale treats a favorable judgment as an independent means of restoring reputation

**Location:** Section 4, Vindication Theory, printed pp. 432 (PDF pp. 16)

Professor Yonathan A. Arbel claims, in “The Credibility Effect: Defamation Law and Audiences” on page 432, that courts and tort theorists justify defamation law partly because it gives a maliciously attacked person a public venue to establish falsity and recover a good name.
